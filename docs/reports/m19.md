# M19 — router improvements, part 3: the stress board, and the geometry that gets rebuilt

M18 found that the function three milestones had called the hot spot costs 0.039
profiled seconds, and that what the `tighten` *stage* actually spends its time on is
building — and discarding — the free space, the triangulation and the via sites that
tightening is done against. This milestone acts on that, in the order the survey
recommended and the owner approved: build the board the work should be measured on,
then take the two cheap candidates, then decide whether the expensive one is still
worth its risk.

Everything below was measured on 2026-08-24 on the development machine (16 cores,
30 GB RAM, Linux 7.0.0-29-generic), CPython 3.14.4, Shapely 2.1.2 / GEOS 3.13.1,
KiCad 9.0.8 — the same machine and the same versions the M16, M17 and M18 figures
were taken on, which is the only reason the wall-clock comparisons mean anything.

The milestone asks for `geometry_for`'s cost to be split between the GEOS set

## 1. M19s — the congestion stress board

### 1.1 What it is

[`examples/backplane`](../../examples/backplane/design.yaml) is a six-slot passive
backplane for a card cage: a 20-way ribbon from the host at the west end, six card
positions on a 22 mm pitch, sixteen pull-ups terminating the bus at the east end, on
a four-layer stackup with a ground plane on In1 and a power plane on In2. 30
components, 18 nets, 168 connections attempted, 190 × 56 mm.

The difficulty is mechanical and it is arithmetic. The cage's card guides sit
between adjacent card positions, they are bolted through the board, and nothing may
pass under one — so each guide leaves a single **4.0 mm channel** across the middle
of the board, and everything on the bus has to use it, seven times over.

* Sixteen signals at 0.25 mm wide with 0.2 mm clearance want **0.45 mm** of any cut
  they cross. VCC and GND want **0.6 mm** each. **Demand at each channel: 8.4 mm.**
* The channel is 4.0 mm. With the guide's own clearance off both sides, the shared
  field scores the cut across it at about **3.8 mm per signal layer — 7.6 mm** across
  the two of them.

Eight point four against seven point six, seven times. It does not fit, and it is
not meant to.

### 1.2 More nets than channels, from the tool

The milestone asks for this to be shown by the capacity model rather than asserted
in prose. The router's own arbiter — `_blocking_cuts` in
[`plan.py`](../../src/aipcb/route/plan.py), charging the same cuts against the same
`LayerField` capacities that
[`check_capacity`](../../src/aipcb/route/check.py) charges declared sketches
against — reports it on every handed-over connection, under `blocked_at` in
`aipcb route all --json`:

```json
{"layer": "B.Cu", "at": [145.0, 128.0], "width_mm": 3.767, "demand_mm": 4.95,
 "over_subscribed": true,
 "nets": ["D1","D10","D11","D2","D3","D7","D8","D9","IRQ","RESET","STROBE"]}
```

**Eleven nets wanting 4.95 mm of a corridor that is 3.767 mm wide.** Across the run
the arbiter marks a cut over-subscribed **132 times**, and `aipcb bench` counts
**130 over-subscribed cuts** on the finished board. Both are the same capacity model
`check_capacity` implements; the difference is only that the arbiter charges the
router's own legs and `check_capacity` charges sketches from `layout.routes`, of
which this design deliberately declares none — declaring them would take the nets
out of the negotiation, which is the one thing this board exists to exercise.

**This is the first time the capacity arbiter has ever refused anything.** M17a
built it against M16a's special-cut model and found *not one* span on the whole
corpus rejected on capacity; length was binding everywhere. It is not binding here.

### 1.3 The honest outcome: it hands over, and says why

| | |
|---|---:|
| connections attempted | 168 |
| routed | **110** (65.5 %) |
| handed over | **58** |
| negotiation iterations | **5** |
| converged | **no** |
| crossings / self-crossings | 0 / 0 |
| copper | 2 690.150 mm |
| vias | 33 |
| DRC | clean on everything it did route |

Every one of the 58 is a `route-handed-over` warning naming the connection, the cut
that stopped it, that cut's width, the demand on it and the nets contending for it.
Nothing is silently dropped.

**Which nets lose is the interesting part, and it is what the design predicted.**
`CLK` is `rip_up: protected` at priority 90; it is routed end to end. Four of the
fifteen ordinary bus lines — `D0`, `D9`, `RESET`, `STROBE` — complete not one hop of
seven. The protection did exactly what the class docstring says it does, on the only
board in this repository with enough pressure to make it matter.

`backplane` is registered in `tests/conftest.py`'s `UNROUTABLE_EXAMPLES` alongside
`overconstrained`, with the reason written out there: it was built to be over
capacity, and a version of it that routed cleanly would have failed at its job.
Everything it *does* route is still DRC-clean, and
`test_routing_is_byte_stable`'s guarantee holds — two consecutive runs produce the
identical board, SHA-256 `ebc7d8a5…`.

### 1.4 The anchor point, published as a miss

M17d fitted `router_seconds` against connections attempted over the eleven boards
and extrapolated 11.3 minutes for a 900-connection board. The whole reason to build
a board at the top of the range is to find out whether that fit means anything out
there. Running M17d's method verbatim over the *eleven original boards* and asking
it to predict this one:

> **Predicted 38.6 s for 168 connections. Measured 137.3 s. A factor of 3.6.**

The fit under-predicts, badly, and the direction is worth naming: **the eleven-board
corpus contained no board that failed to converge in one negotiation iteration**, so
the fit had no way to see the cost of the other four. A power law in connections
alone cannot model a board that routes its connections five times over. §5 re-fits
with this point included and reports what it does to the exponent.

### 1.5 The corpus grows from eleven boards to twelve

The baseline is refreshed in its own commit, per
[`bench/results/README.md`](../../bench/results/README.md)'s rule. Both sets of
totals, so the step is visible and is not mistaken for a regression:

| | eleven boards (`3b59a52`) | twelve boards (`be400c8`) | of which `backplane` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| connections attempted | 278 | 446 | 168 |
| routed | 275 | 385 | 110 |
| copper | 3 897.150 mm | 6 587.300 mm | 2 690.150 mm |
| vias | 81 | 114 | 33 |
| layer changes | 86 | 120 | 34 |
| corpus `router_seconds` | 29.476 | **167.036** | 137.342 |

**The eleven original boards are byte-identical.** `bench --compare` against the old
baseline reports exactly one line — `backplane: not in the baseline` — and no change
to any other board's hash, copper, vias or completion. The new board is 82 % of the
corpus's routing time, which is what a stress board is for and is why **every
percentage in §2 and §3 below is quoted against 167.036 s, not against 29.476 s.**

### 1.6 The multi-sheet linkage, noted and not built

[Multi-sheet schematic output is blocked on a design, not on machinery](../roadmap.md#schematics):
M14a was not built because the largest bundled example had 12 components and the
median 7. `backplane` has 30 components in eight blocks — `slot1` … `slot6` are six
instances of one `bus_slot` module, `term` is a `bus_termination`, and the host
connector and cage supply are the top level. **That is one sheet per block the day
the feature exists, and nothing in the source has to change for it.** This milestone
does not build multi-sheet output and must not; the design it was waiting for now
exists, which is the whole of the note.

## 2. M19a — the point-location tail, vectorised

### 2.1 What changed

`Triangulation.locate_many` batched the R-tree query at M17c and then ran a scalar
Python loop over the candidates it came back with, calling `_inside` — three
`_sign` calls each — once per candidate. M18 counted them: **`_inside` 3 140 252
times and `_sign` 9 426 354 times** on one board.

The three orientations are now computed over whole numpy arrays. The arithmetic is
the same three products subtracted the same way, in the same order, on the same
float64 values, so the signs are bit-identical to the scalar version's and the
verdict is the same verdict; what goes is the interpreter overhead of nine million
two-line function calls.

**The tie-break is the whole risk and it was written to, not hoped for.** Candidates
are tried in ascending triangle index so that a point on a shared edge lands in the
lower-numbered triangle, and a boolean mask does not reproduce that: it says which
candidates contain the point, not which of them has the lowest index. It is a stable
`lexsort` by (point, triangle) and a take-the-first-of-each-run.

`tests/test_routing.py::TestPointLocation` tests it explicitly: a point exactly on
the shared diagonal of two triangles; the same two triangles with their indices
swapped, which must answer the other way; both shared vertices; a point 1e-15 either
side of the diagonal; and — the case that would catch a subtler error — `locate_many`
against the scalar loop it replaced, over every incentre, edge midpoint and vertex of
`mcu-4layer`'s real triangulation. A scratch run outside the repository put 25 500
probes over six jittered meshes through both implementations and `locate`: no
mismatches.

corpus and the board's output is unchanged, so it is recorded here rather than
chased.

### 2.3 It misses its budget, and the reason is the corpus and not the change

**Asked: ≥ 10 % of corpus `router_seconds`. Delivered: 2.9 %.** On the eleven boards
the budget was drafted against, 8.3 % — still short. There is no denominator on
which this candidate clears 10 %.

The reason is visible in the per-board column the milestone asked for. `backplane`
is 82 % of the corpus and improves by 1.8 %, because **point location was never its
cost**: §4.1 has its profile, and 64 % of its routing time is `triangulate_free`.
The boards where point location *was* the cost improve by 8–21 %. M19s landing
first — which the owner's review moved it to the front to achieve — is what put a
board in the corpus whose bottleneck is somewhere else.

Under the [rejection rule](../milestones/m19-incremental-geometry.md#rejection-rule)
a candidate that misses its budget is rejected and its numbers are written into the
survey note's §5, which they have been. **The code is kept, and that is an owner
decision rather than a rule-following one, so it is flagged here rather than
buried.** The case for keeping it: it is forty lines inside one function, it is
strictly faster on eleven of twelve boards and within noise on the twelfth, its
output is byte-identical everywhere, and it removes 12.6 million Python-level calls
from a single predicate. The case against is the rule as written. §6 puts the
question to the owner.

## 3. M19b — bound the private repair field: **rejected**

### 3.1 What was built

`_repair` builds an entire whole-board `LayeredField` for one connection, sixteen
times on `pcie-sata`. Two versions were built and measured.

**The first bounded the free space itself** — clipping the board to the two pads'
bounding box inflated by a fraction of the board's diagonal, with a ladder of
widening attempts ending in the whole board, so that completion could not change by
construction. It is the version the milestone describes.

**The second bounded only the via sites**, keeping the free space and the
triangulation whole-board and re-deriving *only* the candidate site set when the
first attempt failed. This is strictly better on every axis that matters: the
expensive half of the field is paid for once whatever happens; reachability,
components and cut capacities stay whole-board, so a bounded attempt cannot decide a
pad is unreachable when it is not; and the sites it offers are a strict subset of
the whole board's, at the same positions.

The second version needed one clause the milestone does not anticipate, and it is
worth recording because the failure mode is the opposite of the expected one.
**A box that excludes the via a connection wanted does not make the search fail — it
makes it succeed with something absurd.** On `pcie-sata`, `GND/J5.1>J5.MP` went from
29.96 mm with two vias to **158.03 mm with none**: denied the pocket it wanted to hop
through, the search walked the long way round the board on one layer, and because it
*succeeded* nothing ever widened the box. The fix is that a bounded answer is
accepted only if it stays inside the box it was found in; one that wanders outside
is evidence that the bound shaped it, and the bound is lifted. That took
`pcie-sata`'s copper from +124.1 mm to +12.5 mm.

### 3.2 What it measured

Against the twelve-board baseline, with M19a already in:

| | baseline | M19a | M19b (sites, guarded) | vs M19a |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| backplane | 137.342 | 134.520 | 135.577 | +0.8 % |
| enclosure | 2.089 | 1.959 | 1.786 | −8.8 % |
| mcu-4layer | 5.101 | 4.515 | 4.030 | −10.7 % |
| pcie-sata | 16.381 | 14.999 | 13.199 | **−12.0 %** |
| qfn-fanout | 3.039 | 2.853 | 2.963 | +3.9 % |
| usb-port | 1.704 | 1.340 | 1.214 | −9.4 % |
| **corpus, twelve boards** | 167.036 | 161.618 | **160.049** | **−1.0 %** |
| **the eleven original boards** | 29.695 | 27.098 | **24.471** | **−9.7 %** |

Completion unchanged (385/446). Copper **+12.468 mm**, all of it on `pcie-sata`
(1 077.807 → 1 090.275 mm, +1.16 %), in one connection: `GND/U1.17>U1.49` goes from
3.52 mm with no vias to 15.99 mm with two. Ten of twelve board hashes hold;
`enclosure` and `pcie-sata` move.

The free-space-bounding version is worse and is recorded for completeness: corpus
+9.6 % against M19a — because on a board where most repairs *fail*, a ladder that
cannot lose a route pays for the whole-board field anyway and pays for the bounded
one on top. It also lost three connections on `backplane`, since a bounded repair
that succeeds differently changes the board for everything after it.

### 3.3 Why it is rejected

**Asked: ≥ 25 % further, at unchanged completion and unchanged copper.
Delivered: 1.0 % on the corpus, 9.7 % on the eleven boards, and copper moved.**

Three findings, in the order they matter:

1. **M19a and M19b were sized independently against the same profile, and they
   overlap.** M19b's 25 % was priced off `_repair` at 16.30 s of 29.8 s profiled, of
   which `_via_sites` was 12.35 s. M19a took `_via_sites` to 5.22 s *before* M19b
   ran. Most of M19b's prize had already been collected by the candidate scheduled
   ahead of it. Additive budgets against one profile is the mistake, and it is worth
   carrying forward: **the second candidate's budget should be re-priced from the
   profile the first one leaves behind, not from the one that ranked them.**
2. **The corpus's cost has moved somewhere M19b does not reach.** `backplane` spends
   22.9 s of 199 profiled in `_via_sites` and **127.6 s in `triangulate_free`**.
   Bounding via sites cannot touch that, and bounding the triangulation makes it
   worse for the reason in §3.2.
3. **It moves copper, and that is what disqualifies it now rather than merely
   failing it.** The [sequencing rule](../milestones/m19-incremental-geometry.md#sequencing-and-the-fab-round)
   put M19b before the fab round *because* it "keeps completion and copper by
   construction". It does not. A candidate that moves `pcie-sata`'s copper belongs
   after the board is ordered, alongside M19c and M20 — which is where the same rule
   sends everything else that moves copper.

The code is reverted rather than left in the tree. The design is recorded above and
the numbers in the survey note's §5, so that the next person to propose bounding the
repair field meets this measurement rather than re-deriving it. **If it is revived,
revive the site-bounding version with the stays-inside-the-box guard, after the fab
round, and re-price it against the profile of the day.**

## 4. M19c — the gate: **open on the measurement, closed on the sequencing**

### 4.1 The evidence the gate did not have

The gate says: *do not start it if M19a and M19b together land more than half of the
corpus routing time.* They land **2.9 %** — M19a alone, since M19b is rejected.
**On its stated criterion the gate opens, and it opens wider than the survey
expected**, because the board M19s built has a profile the survey never saw.

135.6 s under wrappers):

**Sixty-four per cent of the stress board's routing time is rebuilding the
triangulation, and thirty-nine per cent is one GEOS call inside it.** That is
exactly and only what an incremental mesh replaces. On `pcie-sata` the same split is
6.90 s of 16.6 s; on the board that now dominates the corpus it is 87.1 s of 135.6 s.
**L1's prize is larger than the survey priced it, and the reason it is larger is that
the corpus finally contains a board big enough to show it.**

The 20 : 80 ratio from §0.2 holds here too and matters the same way: the GEOS set
operations are 17.1 s and do not go away — `_via_clears` and `_covered` ask the free
polygon distance-to-boundary questions a mesh answers badly — so the ceiling is the
87 s, not the 104 s of both.

### 4.2 Why it is not started here anyway

The [sequencing the owner settled](../milestones/m19-incremental-geometry.md#sequencing-and-the-fab-round)
is not conditional on the gate:

> 1. **M19s, M19a, M19b** — this milestone's hash-preserving work.
> 2. **A BOM/CPL export milestone, then the fab round.** The board gets ordered.
> 3. **M19c, and any placement implementation from M20, after the board is ordered.**

Step 2 has not happened. M19c can move copper on `pcie-sata` and the rule exists so
that the copper measured and the copper produced are the same copper. **So the gate
is recorded as open and M19c is not started**, which is the same answer the
sequencing gave before the measurement and is now supported by it.

Two things are worth carrying into the milestone that does build it, beyond what the
prompt already says:

* **Re-price it against `backplane`, not against `pcie-sata`.** A 30 % corpus budget
  now means roughly 48 s, and essentially all of it has to come off one board. That
  is a *more* achievable budget than the survey's framing suggested, not a less
  achievable one.
* **The deletion half is not optional here either.** M18 measured 84 obstacle
  disappearances on `pcie-sata`'s F.Cu alone; `backplane` rebuilds 703 triangulations
  against 244, over a repair pass that runs 94 times and fails 58 of them, so the
  sequence of obstacle sets is at least as non-monotone. Either constraint removal
  exists or the rebuild is the documented fallback, and which one must be stated up
  front.

Neither of M19c's two hard conditions — property-based testing of the hand-owned
predicate against shapely including the degenerate classes, and a kill-switch config
with the rebuild path retained and the predicate opt-in — has been touched, because
the candidate has not been started. `hypothesis` is still not a dependency.

## 5. M19d — the extrapolation, re-fitted twice

M17d's method verbatim: **log-log least squares of `router_seconds` against
connections attempted, against total cuts, and against connections × cuts.** Run
from a scratch script outside the repository over committed results files, so the
next re-fit is comparable to this one and not to a remembered number.

**Over the eleven original boards**, which is the line-for-line comparison with M16
and M17:

| Model | M16 | M17 | **M19** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| routing seconds vs **connections** | 1.72 (R² 0.94) | 1.70 (0.94) | **1.67 (0.95)** |
| routing seconds vs total cuts | 2.61 (0.82) | 2.57 (0.81) | **2.55 (0.84)** |
| routing seconds vs **connections × cuts** | **1.12 (0.97)** | **1.10 (0.96)** | **1.09 (0.97)** |
| 900 connections, extrapolated | 23.7 min | 11.3 min | **9.3 min** |

**The exponents did not move. Again.** M17c removed constant factors and left the
shape where it was; M19a removed more constant factors and left it where it was.
Routing cost on these eleven boards is still very close to linear in connections
times triangulation size, which is what "tighten every connection against a
triangulation of the whole board" predicts, and three milestones of constant-factor
work have not touched that. **That is the finding M19c exists to change**, because
changing rebuild-per-connection into update-per-connection is a change to the shape
and not to the constant.

**Over all twelve boards**, which is the better fit and the one to carry forward:

| Model | eleven boards | **twelve boards** |
|---|---:|---:|
| routing seconds vs **connections** | 1.67 (R² 0.95) | **1.87 (0.95)** |
| routing seconds vs total cuts | 2.55 (0.84) | **2.00 (0.88)** |
| routing seconds vs **connections × cuts** | 1.09 (0.97) | **1.03 (0.98)** |
| 900 connections, extrapolated | 9.3 min | **22.5 min** |

Three things fall out of the twelfth point, and they are why the milestone asked for
the fit to be published twice.

1. **The eleven-board fit under-predicts the new board by a factor of 3.9.** It
   expects 34.1 s for 168 connections; the board takes 134.7 s. An extrapolation
   from eleven boards spanning 4 to 90 connections had no way to know that.
2. **The connection exponent rises from 1.67 to 1.87 and the capability-ladder
   number more than doubles, from 9.3 to 22.5 minutes.** The honest reading is not
   that the router got slower — nothing about the eleven boards changed — but that
   the old number was fitted entirely below the range it was being used to predict.
   **22.5 minutes is the figure to carry forward**, and it is still an extrapolation
   from a corpus whose largest board is 168 connections.
3. **The connections × cuts model gets *better*, not worse: R² 0.97 → 0.98, exponent
   1.09 → 1.03.** The twelfth board is the one point most able to break that model
   and it does not break it. Connections times triangulation size remains the thing
   routing cost is close to linear in, and the new board is expensive for exactly
   that reason: 168 connections against 11 776 cuts, re-triangulated 703 times.

One caveat on the connection fit, stated because it is the difference between a
number and a stopwatch reading: **`backplane` does not converge in one negotiation
iteration and every one of the eleven original boards effectively does.** It routes
its connections five times over. A power law in connections alone cannot see that,
so some of the exponent's rise from 1.67 to 1.87 is the corpus acquiring a second
kind of board rather than a bigger one. The connections × cuts fit, which is the
better model on both corpora, is much less disturbed by it.

## 6. What this leaves the owner

Three decisions, and one of them is not urgent.

**1. M19a missed its budget and the code is in the tree.** §2.3 makes the case both
ways. The rejection rule as written says reject; the change is forty lines, is
byte-identical on every board, and is faster on eleven of twelve. Reverting it is a
one-commit operation if the rule is to be applied literally, and the tie-break tests
are worth keeping either way. **This report keeps it and says so out loud rather
than quietly re-reading the rule.**

**2. The budgets in part 3 were priced additively off one profile, and that is why
two candidates missed.** M19b's 25 % was priced off a `_via_sites` that M19a had
already made less than half the size. Whatever milestone builds M19c should re-take
the profile and re-price *after* the candidate before it lands, not before it starts.
§3.3 finding 1.

**3. M19c's prize is bigger than the survey thought, and its budget should be
re-stated against `backplane`.** §4.1. Thirty per cent of the corpus is now about
48 seconds and nearly all of it lives in one function on one board. Nothing about
the risk assessment changes — it is still a hand-owned predicate against ADR 0006
and against the toporouter's cause of death, still conditional on the two hard
conditions the owner added, and still after the fab round.

And one thing that is not a decision: **`examples/backplane` is now the corpus's
centre of gravity, at 83 % of its routing time.** Every future runtime measurement is
mostly a measurement of that board, which is what a stress board is for and is also
a reason to keep quoting per-board numbers next to the corpus percentage. The
milestone asked for that and it is what caught both misses.
