# M16 — toporouter lessons, part 1: delivery report

The [gEDA toporouter postmortem](../notes/toporouter-postmortem.md) read the only
complete open-source rubber-band router that exists, and came back with five
techniques worth taking and three places where this project shares a weakness with
the one that died. This milestone implements the *guards and the instrument*, and
deliberately none of the quality work — because the study's own central finding is
that runtime, not correctness, is what killed the toporouter, and three of the five
techniques trade runtime for quality. Building them unmeasured would have repeated
the autopsy in slow motion.

Everything below was measured on 2026-08-23 on the development machine (16 cores,
30 GB RAM, Linux 7.0.0-29-generic), CPython 3.14.4, Shapely 2.1.2 / GEOS 3.13.1,
KiCad 9.0.8. Where a figure is a convention rather than a measurement it says so in
the same sentence.

## 1. M16a — what `check_capacity` may claim

### 1.1 The measurement came first, and it decided the milestone

The postmortem's candidate C2 did not say "implement special cuts". It said
*measure, and if flip diagonals are rarely the shorter one, scope this as a
correctness tidy-up rather than a quality win*. So the first thing written was not
code, it was a counter.

For every adjacent triangle pair on every layer of every bundled example: does the
quadrilateral admit a second diagonal at all (is it convex), and is that diagonal
*shorter* than the one the CDT drew? The full table is in
[ADR 0014](../decisions/0014-special-cuts.md). The summary is that **between 4% and
29% of diagonals per layer have a shorter partner, and the shortest measured is 2.6%
of the length of the diagonal it pairs with** — a gate the old model priced at its
diagonal's full width that is really a fortieth of it.

That is not a corner case, so resolution 1 was taken: implement.

### 1.2 What was implemented

`triangulate.py::special_cuts()` derives, for each diagonal, the segment joining the
far vertex of each of its two triangles. **Convexity is the soundness condition and
it is checked rather than assumed**: a constrained Delaunay triangulation is free to
leave a pair unflipped precisely because flipping it would put the new diagonal
*outside* the free space, and a segment that leaves the free area is not a cut across
it. Four orientation tests, no geometry library, deterministic by construction.

`check_capacity` charges both families, reports which kind each finding is, and its
diagnostic says what it is describing:

```
X1, X2 together need 0.90 mm of the gap on F.Cu they have to round a corner
through, that is 0.85 mm wide
```

Capacity uses the same convention a CDT diagonal already used — length plus one
clearance — and it is correct for the same reason: the free space is already
inflated by a clearance, so a gate of length *L* holds *n* tracks exactly when *n*
widths and *n*−1 inter-track clearances fit.

### 1.3 The runtime delta, and where it was allowed to land

The milestone permits special cuts to spend runtime because they buy soundness. They
spend very little of it. Best of five, same process:

| | Without | With | Delta |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `check_routes` on `routing-demo` | 638.7 ms | 643.5 ms | **+0.8%** |
| `check_routes` on `usb-port` | 395.1 ms | 398.3 ms | **+0.8%** |
| deriving them, `pcie-sata` F.Cu (866 diagonals) | — | 1.11 ms | — |

Those two examples *are* the measurement, because they are the only bundled designs
that declare `layout.routes`; `check_capacity` returns immediately on a design with
no sketches.

**The router's cost model was not touched**, and that is the milestone's guardrail
rather than an oversight. Charging negotiation for the new cuts changes which
corridor it picks, moves every congestion-sensitive golden file, and trades runtime
for quality — the exact shape of change M16's whole thesis says must be measured
against a baseline first. It is [a roadmap part-2 item](../roadmap.md#part-2-the-quality-candidates-and-the-rule-they-are-held-to)
with a stated budget. ADR 0014 Decision 2.

### 1.4 What the new cuts see that the old ones could not

Every example routed, every finished leg charged to both families:

| Example | CDT cuts | over | Second diagonals | over |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| congestion | 322 | 0 | 218 | 0 |
| diff-pair | 448 | 1 | 317 | **2** |
| ldo-supply | 310 | 3 | 209 | 3 |
| mcu-4layer | 1630 | 2 | 1251 | 1 |
| pcie-sata | 1638 | 25 | 928 | 18 |
| qfn-fanout | 843 | 1 | 561 | 0 |
| routing-demo | 592 | 0 | 466 | **1** |
| usb-port | 490 | 1 | 342 | 0 |

`routing-demo` is the clean demonstration: **nought** over-subscribed diagonals and
**one** over-subscribed second diagonal. The old accounting called that board's
corridors uniformly comfortable, and there is a pinch in it.

### 1.5 The half that would have been done either way

Exposure E1 had two parts and they were always separable. Even with the second
diagonals the cut set is a **subset** — any segment between two obstacle vertices
spanning more than one triangle pair is a cut nothing charges — so the wording is
corrected in all three places it appears: `field.py`'s module docstring,
`check_capacity`'s docstring, and [`docs/topology.md`](../topology.md). All three now
say that this is a **lower bound on congestion, not the criterion in full**, and that
a clean result is evidence rather than proof.

A check that overstates its guarantee is worse than one that states its limits,
because the first kind is trusted.

### 1.6 The test the milestone asked for by name

`TestSpecialCuts::test_the_apex_rounding_wire_is_charged` builds the study's exact
scenario: two triangles sharing a 4 mm diagonal, an apex 0.3 mm above it, and a wire
that enters through one non-diagonal edge and leaves through the other. It asserts
both halves —

```python
assert field_.cuts_crossed(rounding) == []          # the old accounting saw nothing
assert field_.special_cuts_crossed(rounding) == [0] # the new one charges it
```

— because the finding is not "a cut was added", it is "a wire that was charged
nothing is now charged".

## 2. M16b — the other two exposures

### 2.1 E2: the guard that was expected to find nothing, and did not

The note was explicit: *expected result on the current corpus is zero findings, which
is the point.*

It found one. On `examples/pcie-sata`, GND's connection `U1.17>U1.49` — two pads
3.4 mm apart — travels four millimetres east, drops a via, hops to B.Cu for **0.55
mm**, hops straight back to F.Cu, and then retraces its own outbound path in reverse
all the way home. The two F.Cu legs are coincident over most of their length. About
eight millimetres of copper and two vias buy nothing at all.

It is not a geometry defect: every leg is a legal shortest path through its own
sleeve, the board is DRC-clean, and no two nets touch. It is a *search and cost
model* defect — the route changed layer and changed back, so the excursion that
forced the detour bought no layer change — and fixing it is the roadmap's
post-convergence detour pass, which trades runtime for quality and is therefore
gated on §3's harness by decision.

**Two severities, because the two findings are not the same kind of thing.**

| Finding | Severity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `route-crosses-itself` — a leg's polyline is not simple | **error** | A tightened polyline crossing itself is not a design judgement anybody gets to make. No board should carry one. |
| `route-doubles-back` — two legs of one connection meet on one layer away from a join | **warning** | Copper laid twice, not copper in the wrong place. The net is still the net and KiCad's DRC has nothing to say about it, so the honest report is waste. |

Both are centre-line tests rather than buffered-copper tests, deliberately: two legs
that join at a via *do* share copper around the joint, and a buffered test would need
a tuned disc radius at every junction to avoid calling every normal route a defect.
A centre-line that meets another centre-line away from a named endpoint has no
legitimate reading.

The finding is pinned twice — `TestSelfCrossingInvariant::test_the_corpus_carries_exactly_one_known_doubling`
asserts the count is exactly one and names the connection, and
`test_check_loop.py`'s `KNOWN_ISSUES` carries `route-doubles-back` for `pcie-sata`
with the reason. A second occurrence fails the suite.

### 2.2 E3: a premise turned into a dated measurement

The toporouter was killed by the host project converting its base units to
nanometres: coordinates moved into a range the triangulation library's in-circle
predicate had not been tuned for, and edge-flipping recursed ten thousand deep on a
board with two resistors. `CLAUDE.md` exists because of exactly this shape of
failure, and aipcb's own answer — GEOS's CDT rather than a hand-rolled one, snapped
to 1e-6 mm — was, until now, an argument rather than a measurement.

`examples/led-blinker`, routed with `layout.origin_mm` swept, Shapely 2.1.2 /
GEOS 3.13.1:

| Board origin | Segments | Copper | Same board? |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 100 mm (the default) | 46 | 262.722 mm | — |
| 500 mm | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| 1 000 mm | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| **2 147 mm** | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| 5 000 mm | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| 10 000 mm | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| 20 000 mm | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| 50 000 mm | 46 | 262.722 mm | identical |
| **100 000 mm** | **53** | **271.836 mm** | **different** |
| 10⁸ mm | 430 | 262.722 mm | same route, fragmented into ten times the segments |

The bolded rows are the two that matter, and **2 147 mm is where it is because that
is KiCad's own limit** — measured rather than assumed, per `CLAUDE.md`. Board
coordinates are 32-bit nanometres, so ±2 147.483 647 mm is the whole representable
range. Feeding `kicad-cli pcb drc` (KiCad 9.0.8) the boards written at 2 147 mm,
5 000 mm and 100 000 mm, all three report the same coordinate back:

```
"pos": {"x": 2147.483637, "y": -2139.863659}
```

That is INT32_MAX nanometres. **KiCad does not refuse an out-of-range coordinate; it
clamps it, silently**, so the 5 000 mm and 100 000 mm boards are not merely unusual
files, they are files KiCad has quietly flattened (79 and 83 DRC violations
respectively, against a clean board at the default origin).

So the router is exact across *twenty-three times* everything KiCad can represent,
and starts routing differently at forty-seven times it — comfortably outside the
domain in which the question can even be asked. Placement was checked separately at
100 mm and 100 000 mm and is identical relative to the origin, so what moves at
100 000 mm is the router and not the packer.

The premise is now a measurement with a version attached, and the test holds **both
halves**: that the board is identical at 100 mm, 2 147 mm and 50 000 mm, and that it
is *not* identical at 100 000 mm. A premise that says "it works" and never says where
it stops is not measured, it is asserted — and if GEOS ever gets more robust, the
second assertion fails and somebody re-measures rather than silently inheriting a
stale claim.

**One deviation, stated.** The milestone asked for the example to be "translated a
long way from the origin **and scaled**". Scaling a design is not a meaningful
operation here: footprints have fixed physical size, so a scaled board is a
*different* board and cannot be asserted to route identically. What the toporouter's
death was actually about is coordinate magnitude, which translation varies directly
and across five orders of magnitude above. The scale axis was reinterpreted rather
than skipped, and this paragraph is the record of that judgement.

## 3. M16c — the benchmark harness

### 3.1 There was no stage timing to extend

The milestone says to *reuse or extend the existing stage timing*. There was none:
nothing under `src/aipcb/route/` measured time, and the only `time.monotonic` calls
in the package are in the SI runner. `route/timing.py` is new, and it is eleven
stages of `time.perf_counter` and nothing else.

Two properties, both of which are the guardrail rather than taste:

* **Stage boundaries only.** A timer inside the funnel's inner loop or the A*
  relaxation would distort the thing it is describing. Eleven `perf_counter` calls
  against a run that takes seconds is unmeasurable.
* **Off unless somebody asks.** `stages` is `None` on every path that is not a
  benchmark. The router's behaviour is exactly what it was — which the byte-stable
  corpus confirms.

The one judgement call worth naming: `route_design` makes an *exploratory* routing
pass when the board already has copper, to learn which of it is its own from a
previous run. That pass is not timed. Counting it would make a rebuild look slower
than a first build for a reason that has nothing to do with the router.

Shapely 2.1.2 / GEOS 3.13.1, 16 cores, Linux 7.0.0-29-generic.

`overconstrained` routes one of four **by design** — it is four wires that have to
cross in one channel on one layer, and it exists to exercise the hand-over path. The
other ten route to completion.

Reading the columns: *vias* is holes drilled on the board, *hops* is how many times a
connection changed layer (higher when two connections of a net land on the same
hole), *bound* is the fewest hops any router could have made, *over* is
over-subscribed cuts, and *routing s* excludes compile, place and file-writing.

One caveat on *conns*, stated because it flatters the completion column slightly:
the denominator is the one `aipcb route all` prints, which counts the pieces a
pattern generator laid — a fanout escape, a declared pair transition — alongside the
connections the router searched for. Those always succeed. `qfn-fanout` is the board
where this matters most. It is kept that way because a benchmark metric that
disagreed with the number the CLI prints for the same board would be worse than one
that is generous in a stated direction.

### 3.3 What the baseline already says

**Tightening is the router, by wall clock.** Not the search — the geometry:

| Board | tighten | share of routing | field | negotiate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| routing-demo | 0.15 s | 20% | 0.60 s | 0.014 s |
| led-blinker | 0.25 s | 34% | 0.45 s | 0.029 s |
| diff-pair | 0.22 s | 38% | 0.35 s | 0.009 s |
| ldo-supply | 0.19 s | 49% | 0.19 s | 0.011 s |
| congestion | 0.10 s | 56% | 0.07 s | 0.011 s |
| overconstrained | 0.14 s | 71% | 0.06 s | 0.001 s |
| usb-port | 2.35 s | 83% | 0.32 s | 0.143 s |
| mcu-4layer | 8.58 s | 84% | 1.09 s | 0.505 s |
| enclosure | 4.09 s | 89% | 0.33 s | 0.164 s |
| qfn-fanout | 5.00 s | 89% | 0.43 s | 0.191 s |
| **pcie-sata** | **33.98 s** | **94%** | 1.63 s | 0.327 s |

**Negotiated congestion — the part that sounds hard — costs at most 0.51 s on any
board here, and under 0.2 s on eight of the eleven.** On the five largest boards,
83–94% of routing time is in `tighten`. That is a striking echo of the autopsy: Blake spent over a hundred
hours on his geometry pass and it was still producing bugs a year later. This
project's geometry pass is *correct* — one linear funnel sweep with a termination
proof — and it is still where all the time goes, for the entirely different reason
that it runs per connection against a free space that every previously-laid track has
made more complicated.

The practical consequence for part 2 is immediate: **a quality technique that touches
negotiation is nearly free, and one that adds a tightening pass is not.** Spreading
(C1) needs the funnel to run against a nominated point per portal; the detour pass
(C3) re-tightens a subset of routes. Their budgets should be read against a 94%
number, not against a total.

**Runtime growth, which is the graduation condition.** Log-log least squares over the
eleven boards:

| Model | Exponent | R² |
|---|---:|---:|
| routing seconds vs **connections** | **1.71** | 0.94 |
| routing seconds vs total cuts | 2.60 | 0.82 |
| routing seconds vs **connections × cuts** | **1.12** | **0.97** |

The third row is the useful one and it says something mechanical rather than
statistical: routing cost is close to *linear in connections times triangulation
size*, which is exactly what "tighten each connection against a triangulation of the
whole board" predicts. The apparent super-linearity in connections alone is mostly
bigger boards having bigger triangulations.

Extrapolating the connection fit past the measured range — **and it is an
extrapolation, from eleven boards spanning 4 to 90 connections** — a 900-connection
board would take roughly half an hour to route. That is not a comfortable number. It
is also, for the first time, *a number*: the roadmap's graduation condition asked
that "it will not finish" be distinguishable from "it has not finished yet", and it
now is.

**Layer changes nobody asked for.** A via on a connection that never met a corridor
above half capacity is a layer change congestion did not require:

| Board | hops | of which without capacity pressure |
|---|---:|---:|
| pcie-sata | 42 | **16** |
| qfn-fanout | 30 | **12** |
| enclosure | 7 | 5 |
| usb-port | 4 | 2 |
| congestion, mcu-4layer | 1, 5 | 1, 1 |
| the rest | 0 | 0 |

This is the measure-first stage a layer-assignment pass would need, and it says there
is something there — a third of `pcie-sata`'s layer changes and 40% of
`qfn-fanout`'s. It is a proxy, and the roadmap says so: half capacity is a stated
convention, and "no pressure anywhere on the connection" is not the same as "no
pressure at the via". Nobody should build a pass on this number without first
sharpening it.

**Corridor pressure is not a defect count.** `pcie-sata` reports 43 over-subscribed
cuts on a board that is DRC-clean and has no crossings, with F.Cu utilization peaking
at 2.73× capacity. That is the shared, approximate congestion field disagreeing with
the exact per-net stretcher that actually laid the copper — which is what the field
is *for*. The number is useful as pressure: it is where the board is tight, and it is
what a quality change claiming to have made room has to move.

### 3.4 The results file, and what `--compare` treats as a regression

`bench/results/baseline.json` is committed; ad-hoc runs write
`bench/results/<commit>.json` and are git-ignored. The file carries the commit, the
timestamp, and the Python, Shapely and GEOS versions, because wall clock without a
machine attached is not a measurement.

The two thresholds are asymmetric and the asymmetry is the design:

* **Copper length, via and hop counts, completion, crossings and the board hash are
  deterministic.** Same input, same output — so any movement is a real change in
  what the router decided. `--length-threshold` defaults to **2%**, which is not a
  noise tolerance but a statement about how much quality change is worth stopping
  for. A changed hash is reported as a *change*, not a regression: it is news, and it
  is not on its own bad news.
* **Wall clock is not, and never will be.** `--runtime-threshold` defaults to 50%
  for two runs on one machine, and it is applied to `router_seconds` rather than the
  total — because the first board in a run also pays for loading KiCad's footprint
  libraries into a cache the rest of the run reuses, and that one-time cost lands
  entirely in `build`.

### 3.5 The synthetic regression, and CI

The acceptance clause asks that `--compare` be *demonstrated* catching a regression
rather than asserted to. Two tests do it end to end, through the CLI, checking the
exit code a CI job reads:

* `test_compare_catches_a_synthetic_regression` measures `congestion` for real, then
  doctors the baseline to claim half the copper, a tenth of the routing time and one
  fewer via. The real run is then a regression against it on all three counts, and
  the command exits 1.
* `test_compare_catches_a_dropped_connection` uses `overconstrained`, which routes
  one of four by design, and claims in the baseline that it routed all four. The
  comparison has to say the router got worse — which is the shape of failure a
  quality change would actually introduce.

CI gains a `bench` job in the pinned `kicad/kicad:9.0.8` container (it needs the
symbol and footprint libraries, so a bare runner cannot do it). It routes the three
smoke examples and diffs them against the committed baseline with
`--runtime-threshold 400`. Four hundred percent is deliberately useless for fine
timing work: it is comparing against a file measured on a developer's machine, and
its job is to catch an accidental quadratic, not a 10% slip. The deterministic
metrics are compared at their normal thresholds, and those are the ones that will
actually fire.

Measured locally against the committed baseline: **no differences beyond the
thresholds, exit 0.** It has not yet run on GitHub, because nothing has been pushed.

## 4. M16d — the part-2 plan

Written into [`docs/roadmap.md`](../roadmap.md#part-2-the-quality-candidates-and-the-rule-they-are-held-to)
rather than as a milestone file, as asked. Four rules and a table.

**The rules.** Nothing is implemented except against `aipcb bench`, with
before-and-after on the whole corpus in the milestone's own report. Every candidate
carries a runtime budget stated *before* it is built, as a ceiling on
`router_seconds` summed over the corpus. Determinism is not part of the trade —
every candidate stays byte-stable and bounded by iterations rather than wall clock.
And the fourth, which is the one this milestone exists to make possible:

> A candidate that does not pay for itself is **rejected, and its numbers are written
> into the postmortem note** — not deleted, not left in a branch. Recorded, in the
> note whose §C proposed it, as a measured negative result.

That is the closure Blake never got to write. The toporouter's own author had no way
to tell a technique that was worth its cost from one that was not, and nobody after
him could reconstruct the difference; thirty commits over twenty-one months from five
people are all janitorial for exactly that reason.

**The budgets**, as ceilings on corpus `router_seconds`: spreading (C1) +15%, the
detour pass (C3) +30% — which is what Blake's own measurement cost — cluster routing
(C4) +20%, pairwise-conflict ordering (C5) +10% *and it should be negative*, and
charging the second diagonals in the router +10%.

**And the baseline has already half-answered one of them.** C5 proposes a better
first-pass ordering to reduce negotiation iterations. Ten of the eleven examples
converge in **one** iteration and `enclosure` in four, so there is almost nothing
for a better order to save. That is not a refutation — a harder board might behave
differently, and the corpus is small — but it is exactly the kind of thing a
baseline is for, and it moved C5 from "cheap to try" to "read the numbers first".

**Sequencing.** Part 2 comes after the `pcie-sata` fab round, **by decision rather
than by dependency**. That board is the first thing this project will have
fabricated, and a fabrication result is evidence nothing in the corpus can supply.
Moving the router's output underneath it while boards are in flight would mean the
copper that was measured and the copper the tool now produces are not the same
copper.

**Via minimization** is deliberately not a candidate of its own. §3.3 measured it
instead: 90 layer changes corpus-wide against a bound of 14, of which 37 were made
without capacity pressure. Whether that is worth a pass is now a question with
numbers attached, and C3 and C4 both reduce vias as a side effect anyway. The
roadmap says to read the table before proposing it.

## 5. The instruction that could not be followed as written

The acceptance list asks that *"the layer-assignment candidate file"* be updated to
point at bench for its stage 1. **There is no such file.** The postmortem names five
techniques (C1–C5) plus the benchmark (C6) and two — now three — flagged exposures;
layer assignment appears in it only as a 1992 Dayan & Dai technical report in the
sources table, marked *abstract only, full text paywalled*. Nothing in
`docs/roadmap.md`, `docs/decisions/` or `docs/notes/` carries a layer-assignment
candidate.

The instruction's *intent* is clear and is discharged: the point was to record that
the benchmark subsumes a measure-first stage so that nobody later builds
`route --report-quality` as a separate command. That is now stated in two places — in
the roadmap's toporouter section, and in `bench.py`'s own module docstring, which
names `layer_changes_without_pressure` as that measurement. If a layer-assignment
candidate is ever written up, §3.3's table is its stage 1 and it should start by
sharpening the proxy rather than re-measuring from scratch.

## 7. What this milestone did not do, and why

**No quality technique from §C.** Not spreading, not the detour pass, not cluster
routing, not pairwise ordering — and not charging the second diagonals inside the
router's cost model, which is the one that was most tempting because the cuts were
right there. The reason is the study's own central finding, and it is worth stating
plainly one more time: the gEDA toporouter was better than the incumbent maze router
on quality nearly everywhere, and it died anyway, because it was three to twenty
times slower and nobody had a benchmark to see it coming. This milestone built the
benchmark before spending anything on quality, which is the whole of its argument.

**No fix for the doubled-back GND connection on `pcie-sata`.** It is now visible,
pinned, and named as the detour pass's first concrete customer. Fixing it means
changing the search or the cost model, which needs the harness that landed in the
same milestone to justify itself against.

**The remaining gap in the cut set is named, not closed.** Second diagonals make
`check_capacity` tighter, not complete. A segment between two obstacle vertices
spanning more than two triangles is still a cut nothing charges, and if a board is
ever found that this check passes and no router can build, ADR 0014 is where that
conversation starts.
